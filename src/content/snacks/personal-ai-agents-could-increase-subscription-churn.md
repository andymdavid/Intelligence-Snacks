Many subscription businesses benefit from inertia. Joining can take one click, while leaving may involve several screens, emails and repeated prompts to stay. That imbalance matters because customers often continue paying for services they rarely use, not because they've actively decided to keep them, but because cancelling demands time and attention.

A personal AI agent could absorb that offboarding work. Before a long holiday, for example, someone could ask an agent to cancel Netflix and resubscribe when they return. The same approach could apply whenever a service is useful only intermittently. If opting out and back in becomes effortless, customers can align payments more closely with periods of actual use, turning a continuous subscription into a more episodic purchase.

The practical rhythm would still be set by billing periods, so this isn't a matter of switching access on and off each day. Providers might also try to block agent-led cancellations, although that becomes difficult when an agent uses the same browser interface as a person. Even within those limits, agents that persist through offboarding friction could make churn less predictable and remove a quiet source of recurring revenue.
