Pete calls it the “slop horizon”, the point where software can be built faster than someone can use it, test it and check its direction. In his view, AI can keep adding features beyond that point. The resulting systems can still be useful, but they may lack the care that comes from regular use.

That doesn’t necessarily mean reading the code. Pete describes using a product, spotting a feature he never touches and stripping it out. Jarrad does the same with his own tools, which he uses every day. He also tunes small details as he goes; knowing what good looks like, he says, helps him move quickly.

Jarrad thinks people will still pay for software, even if it becomes cheaper to build. He would rather pay a team focused on one problem than rely on an internal team whose attention is split between its own work and maintaining software. Pete sees a similar reason to pay. Someone has spent time using the product and deciding what it needs.
