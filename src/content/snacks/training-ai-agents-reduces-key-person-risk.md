AI agents can work around the clock and follow instructions, but capability doesn't remove the need for management. Pete compares them to talented new graduates who can do a great deal once they understand how a business operates. They still need onboarding, clearly defined expectations and regular check-ins, just as human employees do.

The investment pays off when training becomes part of the operating system. Guardrails, documented processes and tasks that are narrow enough to execute well give agents a reliable way to work. Repeated use then improves those instructions and builds experience into the process. Unlike a trained employee, the agent won't get bored, leave for another company and take that accumulated know-how with it.

This exposes a familiar weakness in many businesses. A senior employee may be valuable because they carry years of operational knowledge in their head, but that also makes their continued availability a key-person risk. Writing down how work gets done makes the knowledge available to colleagues and agents alike. Training AI agents therefore forces a useful discipline, turning personal expertise into records, rules and processes the organisation can keep using.
