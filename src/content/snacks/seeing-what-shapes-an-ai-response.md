Pete ran DeepSeek on his own machine and asked it about Tiananmen Square. He put an Amnesty International write-up into the system prompt and said he could sometimes coax the model into acknowledging events. When he asked for an opinion on the government, he saw its streamed thinking get partway through before disappearing. The response that followed was uncertain. Running the model locally had not removed his concern about what shaped its answers.

Mark separates that question into layers. There is the model's training, including its data and biases, and then there is the software that serves it to users. He wants more visibility into both. A provider can put instructions around a model that users cannot see, while a visible system prompt still leaves questions about the model underneath it. His proposed response is open models, open datasets and open software around them.

Maple tries to keep that surrounding layer thin, Mark says. He describes its code and system prompts as open source, with minimal instructions that identify the assistant as Maple. Users can inspect those instructions and decide whether they agree with them. As Maple develops its own agent, he says its memory system will also be open so people can see how their conversations are catalogued and recalled.
