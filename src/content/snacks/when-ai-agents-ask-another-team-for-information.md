Pete describes Wingman agents working within scopes such as a project or a DevOps function. People in a scope can get access to a key that decrypts its information. He would give different agents the information they need for their work, so an agent in one part of the business would not automatically know everything held elsewhere. Mark asks what happens when those agents are allowed to talk to each other.

He uses Apple's secrecy as an example. An employee does not get access to every project, and approval might cover only the piece they need to know. In a company using agents, he imagines someone repeatedly pushing their agent to fetch information from another team until it complies. He also raises impersonation. An agent could claim to be a person who is entitled to ask, making the request look legitimate.

Pete says Wingman requires signed requests for access to a record, followed by decryption. Once an agent has the information in plain text, however, he says the problem remains. He would limit what it can access and the tools it has for keeping or sharing data. One possibility they discuss is giving it a narrow output, such as JSON that a script interprets, rather than tools that let it take any action on the computer.
