The better an AI system becomes at routine work, the more difficult each remaining human interaction becomes. Boilerplate, repetitive checks and straightforward execution disappear into agents and workflows. What reaches a person is the exception that the system could not resolve, often a decision with real consequences. Automation therefore does not simply reduce work. It concentrates human attention on the hardest cases and removes the easier tasks that once gave the day some cognitive relief.

That changes the design of the handoff. When a person is needed, the system should preserve their limited attention by making the decision as easy as possible to understand. Paul suggests presenting the same underlying work as a podcast, video or diagram when those formats communicate it better than a wall of text. The format is not decoration. It determines how quickly someone can recover the context, identify the unresolved point and make a sound judgement.

Paul imagines a cadence closer to high intensity training than a conventional desk day. A person might focus fully for ten minutes in an hour, then step away while agents continue working and return when the next difficult decision is ready. That could mean walking, exercising or fishing with a notification waiting on a phone. The unresolved challenge is preventing automation from turning every hour into eight hours of uninterrupted high intensity work.
